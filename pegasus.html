    Byte-Sized Breaches · Case file 02 · 2016–2023

    Pegasus

    A spy program that could quietly take over a phone, read every message and switch on the microphone, sometimes without its owner tapping anything at all.

    It was sold to governments to catch criminals and terrorists. Investigators kept finding it on the phones of journalists, activists, lawyers and politicians. This is how it worked, and how it was caught.

    Chapter 1 · Many doors

    Your phone has more doors than you think

    In Greek myth, Pegasus was a winged horse that could fly over any wall. The spyware named after it was built to do the same to phones.

    Think of your phone as a house with many doors. Every app that receives something from outside (a message, a photo, a call) is a door. Behind each one, a bit of code opens whatever arrives.

    Some doors stay shut until you open them. Others open by themselves the moment something arrives. Guess which is which.

      Go deeper · Zero-days and who sells them

      Every automatic door is part of the phone's attack surface: code that runs on data a stranger chose. If that code has a bug, the stranger may be able to take control of it.

      Pegasus needed bugs the phone's maker didn't know about yet. These are called zero-days, because the maker has had zero days to fix them. Working zero-days for iPhones and Android phones are rare and expensive.

      Pegasus is made by NSO Group, an Israeli company founded in 2010. NSO says it sells only to government agencies, to fight terrorism and serious crime. In 2016 The New York Times reported that NSO charged $650,000 to hack 10 iPhones, on top of a $500,000 setup fee.

      Chapter 2 · One tap · August 2016

      The text message that wanted one tap

      In August 2016, Ahmed Mansoor, a well-known human rights activist in the United Arab Emirates, received two text messages from numbers he didn't know. They promised new secrets about prisoners tortured in Emirati jails. Each ended with a link.

      Mansoor had been targeted by spyware before. What would you do?

      Go deeper · Trident and the million-dollar dissident

      Citizen Lab, a research group at the University of Toronto, worked with the security company Lookout to analyse the attack. They called their report "The Million Dollar Dissident", because governments had already spent heavily on spyware to target Mansoor in earlier years.

      The three bugs were CVE-2016-4657 (memory corruption in WebKit, Safari's engine), CVE-2016-4655 (a kernel information leak that defeated memory shuffling, known as KASLR) and CVE-2016-4656 (kernel memory corruption that gave full control, a so-called jailbreak).

      The link pointed to a website run on NSO Group's infrastructure, with a name made to look harmless. The example link above is invented.

      Apple fixed all three bugs in iOS 9.3.5 on 25 August 2016.

      Chapter 3 · Zero clicks · May 2019

      The call you can't refuse

      Relying on a tap is risky for the attacker: the target might not fall for it, and might report it. So the attackers went after the doors that open by themselves.

      In 2019 they used a bug in WhatsApp's calling feature. To make your phone ring, the app first swaps a little data with the caller to set up the call. A booby-trapped call could break in during that setup, before you had decided anything.

      Go deeper · The WhatsApp bug and the lawsuit

      The bug, CVE-2019-3568, was a buffer overflow in WhatsApp's calling code (its VoIP stack). Specially crafted call-setup packets let the attacker run code inside the app, from where Pegasus could be installed.

      Some targets did see a missed call from a strange number, which then disappeared from their call log.

      WhatsApp sued NSO Group in October 2019. In December 2024 a US judge ruled that NSO was liable for hacking WhatsApp users. In 2025 a jury ordered NSO to pay WhatsApp more than $167 million; a judge later reduced the amount and barred NSO from targeting WhatsApp.

      Chapter 4 · Zero clicks · 2021

      A computer hidden inside a picture

      In 2021, Citizen Lab examined the iPhone of a Saudi activist and found something stranger. Attackers had sent iMessages with files named like GIF animations. They were really PDF documents, and iMessage opened them automatically to show a preview.

      Inside was an image in JBIG2, an old compression format for black-and-white scans. JBIG2 can combine strips of pixels with simple rules called AND, OR and XOR. Google's security team later found that the attackers had chained about 70,000 of these steps into a tiny working computer, all inside one image.

      That sounds impossible, so try it yourself. Pick the rule that turns rows A and B into the target.

      Go deeper · FORCEDENTRY

      Citizen Lab named the exploit FORCEDENTRY. The bug, CVE-2021-30860, was an integer overflow in the JBIG2 decoder in Apple's CoreGraphics, which let the image write outside its own memory.

      The attackers then used JBIG2's pixel operations as logic gates, built adders and other circuits from them, and used that improvised computer to do the calculations they needed to take over the process. Google Project Zero called it one of the most technically sophisticated exploits it had ever seen.

      Apple fixed it in iOS 14.8 on 13 September 2021, and in November 2021 sued NSO Group. (Apple dropped the lawsuit in 2024.)

      Chapter 5 · Inside the phone

      It reads over your shoulder

      Once in, Pegasus holds the phone's master keys. It can read messages, emails and photos, track where the phone goes, and switch on the microphone and camera.

      What about encrypted apps like Signal or WhatsApp? Encryption scrambles a message while it travels, like a sealed envelope. But on your own screen, you write and read in plain text. Pegasus doesn't need to open the envelope. It reads over your shoulder.

      Go deeper · Built to stay hidden

      Lookout's 2016 analysis listed apps whose data Pegasus could collect, including WhatsApp, Telegram, Viber, Skype, Gmail and Facebook, along with texts, calls, contacts, calendar and location.

      Pegasus showed no icon and nothing on screen. It could also delete itself if it risked being discovered.

      Because it works on the device itself, end-to-end encryption doesn't stop it. Encryption still matters, though: it stops mass snooping on networks, which forces attackers to hack phones one at a time, at great cost.

      Chapter 6 · The Pegasus Project · July 2021

      50,000 numbers on a list

      In 2021, a leaked list of more than 50,000 phone numbers reached Forbidden Stories, a Paris-based journalism nonprofit, and Amnesty International. Investigators believe the numbers were picked as possible Pegasus targets by NSO's government clients from 2016 onwards.

      Working with 17 news organisations, they called the investigation the Pegasus Project. A number on the list didn't prove the phone was hacked. So Amnesty's Security Lab examined phones one by one.

      Among the phones examined was that of Hatice Cengiz, fiancée of Saudi journalist Jamal Khashoggi. Amnesty found traces of Pegasus on it from the days after Khashoggi was murdered in October 2018.

      Go deeper · Not the only one

      Amnesty published its methods and a free tool, the Mobile Verification Toolkit (MVT), so other researchers could check phones themselves.

      iPhones keep more of the logs investigators rely on than most Android phones. So a "no traces found" result, especially on Android, doesn't prove a phone was never infected.

      Pegasus has competitors. In 2022, a similar spyware called Predator was found on the phone of Greek journalist Thanasis Koukakis, part of a surveillance scandal that shook the Greek government.

      Chapter 7 · Catching it

      Footprints of an invisible spy

      How do you catch software built to be invisible? Like a burglar, it leaves footprints. Investigators search a phone's logs for traces that don't belong.

      One clue: Pegasus ran hidden programs with names chosen to blend in with the real parts of iOS. Below are programs recorded running on a phone. Most are genuine iPhone system processes. Three are names that Amnesty's investigators linked to Pegasus. Flag them.

        Go deeper · How the real checks work

        Names alone aren't proof. Investigators combine them with timestamps, records of which programs used mobile data, crash reports, and lists of web addresses known to belong to Pegasus servers.

        In 2024, Kaspersky described another clue: traces left in a log iPhones write when they shut down.

        Since November 2021, Apple has sent "threat notifications" to people it believes were targeted by state-sponsored spyware. Several Pegasus cases were confirmed after someone received one and asked for help.

        Chapter 8 · Defence

        What actually helps

        Pegasus is expensive and aimed at a small number of people: journalists, activists, lawyers, politicians and diplomats. You are very unlikely to be one of its targets. But the habits that make its job harder also protect you from everyday attacks.

        First, a quick test. For each attack in this story, could the target have stayed safe just by not tapping anything?

        Game 8 · Could you have dodged it?

        Everyone

        Update right away

        Every attack in this story stopped working once its fix shipped. Spyware makers count on people who put off updates.

        Everyone

        Restart regularly

        Recent versions of Pegasus often didn't survive a restart on iPhones, so attackers had to break in again, which risks getting caught. Restarting every few days costs nothing.

        Everyone

        Think before you tap

        One-click attacks still exist, and most everyday scams arrive as links. Be wary of urgent messages from strangers.

        At risk

        Turn on Lockdown Mode

        Apple's Lockdown Mode, added in 2022, shuts many of the doors that open by themselves. Citizen Lab said it blocked the 2023 BLASTPASS attack. Android 16 has a similar Advanced Protection mode.

        At risk

        Take warnings seriously

        If Apple or Google warns you about state-sponsored attackers, believe it. Contact a free helpline such as Access Now's Digital Security Helpline, or Amnesty's Security Lab.

        At risk

        Keep sensitive talks off the phone

        No phone setting can promise safety from a well-funded attacker. For the most sensitive conversations, meet in person and leave phones in another room.

        Go deeper · BLASTPASS, the 2023 attack

        In September 2023, Citizen Lab found another zero-click iMessage exploit on the phone of someone working at a civil society organisation in Washington, D.C. It hid booby-trapped images inside PassKit attachments, the format used for tickets and boarding passes.

        Apple fixed the bugs (CVE-2023-41064 and CVE-2023-41061) in iOS 16.6.1 within days, and confirmed that Lockdown Mode blocked the attack.
